# CD-024 — The modern English village is ERA's architectural baseline

**Date:** 2026-07-26
**Status:** PROPOSED — held open by Roy's direction, 2026-07-26: *"I also
wouldn't canonize CD-024 yet. … We have found the era, not the voice."*
**Domain:** Design — architecture / visual language

## The decision

ERA's architecture is a **modern English village of the late 1970s and early
1980s** — red brick, white render, timber accents, low-rise, some flat roofs and
some pitched, modern but warm. This **replaces** the previous architectural
direction rather than joining it.

## Roy's review of Sheet 01 (2026-07-26)

The era reads; the language is coherent; the sheet format is kept. But the work
still looks like *"an architectural exercise, not ERA"* — it could belong to a
city builder or a transport sim. Verdict: **~60–70% there. The era is found; the
voice is not.** Direction: push proportions 10–15% toward charm, more generous
overhangs, bigger windows, friendlier entrances, softer roof edges, chunkier
geometry, more colour on awnings/doors/balconies, less orthogonal perfection.
The next board is therefore not more buildings — it is the identity hunt.

## Alternatives considered

- **Amend the existing Museum in place.** Rejected: the previous direction drifts
  toward historic English village vocabulary (Tudor/Victorian/Cotswolds
  adjacency) that the brief explicitly forbids. Patching it would have preserved
  the ambiguity.
- **Produce buildable geometry immediately.** Rejected by Roy mid-exercise: the
  objective is to *discover* the strongest language, not to produce assets.
  Buildability is deliberately not optimized for at this stage.

## Why this won

The 1970s/80s window gives ERA an architecture that is unmistakably English and
unmistakably *modern* — optimistic, ordinary, warm — without the heritage
signalling that pulls a stylized game world toward chocolate-box nostalgia. It
also supplies a genuinely restricted material palette (one brick, one render, one
timber, one tile) that geometry-led stylization needs in order to read.

## What it rejects or supersedes

- Medieval, Tudor, Victorian and Cotswolds vocabulary — excluded outright.
- The previous Museum's architectural collections as the governing baseline.

## Consequences

- The twelve concepts on Sheet 01 define the vocabulary: how a wall meets a roof,
  where a fascia goes, how an accent colour is spent, how corners soften.
- Rules held across all twelve: matte only, no gloss; geometry carries the
  detail, textures stay flat; one brick, one render, one timber, one tile; a deep
  painted fascia wherever a roof stops; one accent colour per building, used once
  and used hard; soft corners everywhere; generous openings.
- The **voice** was hunted on Sheet 02. Lead's read: between S-06 (+20) and
  S-07 (+30), carried by S-09's built-by-hands imperfection. Sheets 03–05
  proceeded on the locked S-06/S-07 midpoint per Roy's brief.
- The **lovability layer** (Sheet 03): charm as reversible acts of care.
  Lead's verdict: L-02 Front Door, L-03 Window Boxes, L-06 Craft; recommended
  **L-12 Lived-In**. Every element a per-resident variable (→ CD-009, CD-025).
- The **street language** (Sheet 04): lead's verdict ST-02 Avenue, ST-10 Pocket
  Square, ST-07 Play Street; recommended **ST-12 The ERA Street** — the street
  supplies what the buildings withhold (green, ground pattern), repeats their
  materials at ground pitch; the street frames, the houses speak. Negative
  finding kept: all-brick ground (ST-04) swallows the brick buildings.
- The **places** (Sheet 05): lead's verdict PL-01 Bakery Step, PL-08 Small
  Bridge, PL-02 Old Oak; recommended **THE OLD OAK as the emotional centre of
  every ERA town** — it precedes the town, football touches it without owning
  it, grief and celebration share it, and its meaning accumulates physically
  (the slowest clock of CD-025). Place rules: no monuments; the ground
  remembers — worn paths are the town's handwriting.
- Production (geometry, kits, LODs) is deliberately deferred until the language
  is locked.
